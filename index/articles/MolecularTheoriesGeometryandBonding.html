Molecular Theories: Geometry and Bonding

Intermolecular Forces of Attraction (IMFA)

Intermolecular Forces of Attraction are the forces in molecule bonds that hold them together. There are many types, with the most well-known being Hydrogen bonds, Ion-Dipole, Ion-Induced, and Van der Waals forces (Keesom/Dipole-Dipole, Debye/Dipole-Induced, London-Dispersion).

Hydrogen Bonds

Hydrogen (H) bonds with Oxygen (O), Nitrogen (N), or Fluoride (F) - Because of the close proximity between atoms, they tend to be very close together.

Examples: H₂O (water), HF (hydrogen fluoride), C₂H₅OH (ethanol), NH₃ (ammonia), etc.

Ion-Dipole Forces

Ion + Polar Element - The ion attracts the polar molecule.

Examples: NaCl (salt), HCl (hydrochloric acid), HF (hydrogen fluoride), etc.

Ion-Induced Dipole Forces

Ion + Non-polar Element - The ion disrupts the non-polar element's electron cloud. Stronger than Dipole-Induced forces because ions have stronger charges compared to polar molecules.

Examples: HbH⁺ + O₂ → HbO₂ + H⁺ (oxygen transport in hemoglobin), NO₃⁻ + I₂ (nitrate & iodine), etc.

Dipole-Dipole Forces

Polar Element + Polar Element - Like magnets, opposite sides attract.

Examples: HCl (hydrochloric acid), H₂O (water), SO₂ (sulfur dioxide), HF (hydrogen fluoride), etc.

Dipole-Induced Dipole Forces

Polar Element + Non-Polar Element - The polar element disrupts the non-polar element's electron cloud.

Examples: H₂O + I₂ (water + iodine), H₂O + O₂ (water + oxygen), HCl + Xe₂ (hydrochloric acid + xenon), etc.

London-Dispersion Forces

Fluctuating Dipole + Induced Dipole - All atoms have dipole moments, they're just not permanent. Random fluctuations in electron density cause this to happen.

Examples: He, Ne, Ar, Kr (noble gases), F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ (diatomic halogens), O₂, H₂ (atmospheric gases), etc.

Summary of Intermolecular Forces

Force Type Description Energy (kJ/mol)
Hydrogen Bonds H bonds with O, N, or F - very close atomic proximity 10-40
Ion-Dipole Ion + Polar Element attraction 40-600
Ion-Induced Dipole Ion disrupts non-polar element's electron cloud 3-15
Dipole-Dipole Polar Element + Polar Element (magnetic attraction) 5-25
Dipole-Induced Dipole Polar element disrupts non-polar element's electron cloud 0.5-2
London-Dispersion Fluctuating dipole + induced dipole (electron density fluctuations) 0.05-40

Molecular Orbital Theory

Molecular Orbital Theory puts an orbital around the whole molecule. The theory states that instead of electrons being connected to individual atoms, they follow a shape based on what the atomic nuclei do. Linear Combinations of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO) gives these shapes names. MO Theory and VB Theory stand side by side as founding fathers of quantum chemistry.

Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

The Valence Bond Theory puts an orbital in the atom and connects them into the web of chemical bonds in a molecule. If these bonds have overlapping orbitals, they are called Sigma-Pi bonds:

  • Sigma bonds = Two orbitals / one pair
  • Pi bonds = 3+ orbitals / two+ pairs

Because the theory is localized to the atoms involved, it's easier to visualize chemical reactions, breaking bonds, and electron movement.

VBT also helps determine magnetic properties and stability of complex compounds using the hybridization of the central metal ion along with molecular structure. VBT explains covalent bonding and its properties beyond just structure, giving reason to why atoms bond in the first place.

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) Theory

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion Theory models the geometrical structure of a molecule depending on the amount of Valence Shell Electron Bond Pairs along its atoms. The model assumes these electron pairs arrange themselves to minimize repulsion effects for stability.

Finding molecular geometry is important because it determines physical, chemical, and biological activity. How do you find molecular geometry using VSEPR Theory?

  1. Draw the Lewis Structure
  2. Get the steric number (sum of bonded atoms and lone pairs, or electron regions)
  3. Space the pairs/regions as far apart as possible for least repulsion (this is why bond angles are useful)
  4. Use the steric number to find the base geometry via the AXE method

The steric number determines the molecular geometry.

Sigma vs Pi Bonds

If two orbitals overlap, they form pi bonds. The presence of pi bonds 'locks' atoms in the same plane, making their configuration unchangeable. Sigma bonds (between 3+ overlapping orbitals) can twist into any shape, conformation, or configuration.
